An AI photoshoot turns ordinary product photos into on-model catalog images. You photograph the garment once, on a hanger, a mannequin or a person, and the studio produces a set of frames that look like one planned shoot: the same model, the same set, the same light.

This guide explains what happens between the upload and the finished frames, so you know what to expect before you spend a credit.

## What you upload

Two photos are required: the **front** and the **back** of the garment. Everything else is optional, and each extra photo removes a guess:

- A fabric or embroidery close-up, so texture and thread work are read from a real photo.
- Bottom wear, when the product is a set.
- A mannequin or flat-lay shot, which shows the true length and shape.
- A model face, if you want the same face across your catalog.
- A style reference, which sets the room, the floor and the lighting.

Phone photos work. The wine kaftan on our home page was uploaded as five phone photos taken against a workshop wall.

## What the studio does with them

The shoot runs in six steps.

1. **Upload.** The photos are stored with the role you gave each one.
2. **Analyse.** One vision pass reads the garment: category, main colour, fabric, print, pattern, neckline, embroidery. That record is what every later frame answers to.
3. **Plan.** Six poses are planned for this product. If your style reference shows a person, the fourth pose takes their posture and camera angle.
4. **Generate.** The first frame is made first. It fixes the model, the set and the light, and the other five follow on the same set.
5. **Check.** Each image is compared with your product photos for shape, colour, material, logo and details. A mismatch is archived and that pose is generated again.
6. **Deliver.** The frames land in your history, and every version is kept.

## What comes back

Every shoot delivers the same six frames in the same order: a full front, a three-quarter turn, the back, a signature pose, a face and details frame, and a close-up of the product's most distinctive feature. ## How to judge an AI photoshoot

The risk with any generated image is that it looks good and shows the wrong product. A customer who receives something different from the photo sends it back. Check four things on every result:

- **Length and silhouette.** Does the hem fall where it does on the real garment?
- **Colour.** Compare against the product in daylight, not against the screen alone.
- **Print and embroidery.** Count the motifs and look at their placement.
- **The back.** The back view should match your back photo: neck shape, ties, closures and seams.

In the wine kaftan shoot, the first three-quarter frame drifted from the garment's real length. The product check caught it, that attempt was archived, and the pose was generated again. That is the behaviour to look for in any tool: a failed frame should be replaced or flagged, never passed off as finished.

## When an AI catalog shoot fits

It fits when you need consistent listing images for many SKUs and you already have clear product photos. It is not a replacement for a brand campaign with a story, a location and a stylist. [AI catalog shoot or studio shoot](/blog/ai-catalog-shoot-vs-studio-shoot) compares the two in more detail.
